My primary resarch interest is the evolution and properties of circadian clocks, which are found in most organisms and influence most aspects of physiology, metabolism, and behavior. This has profound consequences because it means that organismal responses to environemntal variation are shaped by (potentially quite complex) interactions between external factors (light, temperature, etc.) and internal rhythms. Circadian biology is highly relevant to human health (because circadian disruptions are linked to all sorts of metabolic, cardiovascular, and psychological problems) and to the effects of climate change on natural ecosystems (because light pollution, global warming, and other human impacts are changing temporal patterns of environmental variation).

I am interested in how circadian clocks operate and evolve outside of the handful of well-studied model systems that have been the focus of most circadian research. In particular, cnidarians are an important and under-utilized study system in circadian biology because 1) their evolutionary position is key to understanding how clocks first arose and subsequently diversified in animals; and 2) their simple body plans and lack of a central nervous system make it is easier to study the physiological principles of circadian rhythms. 


## Current projects

### 1. Effects of circadian rhythms on regeneration

Studies in many different systems and taxa have identified links between circadian rhythms (or light cycles, more generally) and rates of wound healing and regeneration. This can have profound consequences, as the timescale of wound healing can vary significantly depending on the time of day the injury first occured. However, we don't yet understand the mechanisms underlying this effect, nor how it interacts with more extreme regeneration programs that involve regrowing body parts.

_Nematostella vectensis_ is an excellent system to tackle this question because it is a good mdoel for both circadian biology and whole-body regeneration. I am currently characterizing the effects of various circadian manipulations on regeneration and investingating whether this may be due to 1) general metabolic effects of circadian rhythms; or 2) direct circadian control of cell proliferation following injury. 


### 2. Circatidal rhythms in mussels

People often think of the deep sea as a homogeneous, static environment. However, every time we look, we find more diversity and complexity in these ecosystems. One fun example is the existence of deep tides, which cause changes in seawater properties at hydorthermal vents on a predictable tidal cycle. Our collaborators have described circatidal rhythms in deep-sea mussels inhabiting these vents, showing that biological rhythms are important even in seemingly alien environments. However, we do not yet know the organismal consequences or mechanisms of these deep-sea rhythms. As part of this collaboration, we are studying shallow-water relatives of the deep-sea mussels to compare their physiological rhythms and repertoires of light-sensing proteins. 

### 3. Role of rhythmicity in larval behavior

Invertebrate larvae face the important task of settling in a habitat that can support a successful adult life. To optimize survival and settlement, larvae sense their environment and actively maneouver, and we know that that light cues, in general, are important for larval beahvior. We also know that internal circadian rhythms begin soon after birth/hatching in model systems (think _Drosophila_ and mice), at least at the molecular level. However, we don't knnow whether this also occurs in cnidarians or other marine larvae. I am interested in testing whether larvae possess intrinsic circadian rhyhtms and, if so, what the effects of light cycles and circadian disruptions are on larval beahvior, which I am addressing using behavior tracking experiments.
