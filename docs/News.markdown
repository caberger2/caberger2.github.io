# Check out my new paper on adaptive molecular convergence over deep time

I am extremeley pleased (and a little relieved!) to see the culmination of my postdoctoral work published in _PNAS_! [You can read the open access article here.](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2616248123) 

<img src="{{ '/assets/images/AMC_figure.webp' | relative_url }}" alt="Figure 1 from Berger et al., 2026" width="800">

I'm very grateful to the excellent team of co-authors, including members of the Cartwright, Miglietta, and Varney labs, and I would also like to highlight the contrbutions of my talented undergraduate student, Sam! This project ended up taking a very different path than we originally expected. I started off with the goal of improving and time-calibrating the phylogeny of Medusosozoa (jellyfish and friends)---as the deep relationships in this group have proven difficult to resolve---and then using this tree to analyze patterns of convergent character evolution, particularly eyes (which we know originated many times in this group, thanks to [Natasha Piccinai's great paper](https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(18)30691-2)).

Along the way, we used a new tool to analyze what we call adaptive molecular convergence---genes that have an excess of convergent amino acid substitutions, consistent with positive selection---published by [Fukushima and Pollock](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41559-022-01932-7), and found that there was a bunch of it, even between jellyfish that diverged ~600 million years ago. In fact, species with prominent convergent phenotypes do not appear to be in any way special when it comes to genome-wide levels of adaptive molecular convergence. I won't summarize the whole paper, but we argue that genomic convergence is predominantly shaped by many unobserved selective forces, rather than one or a few convergent phenotypes of large effect, and that the result is a non-negligible amount of convergence even between very distantly related genomes.
